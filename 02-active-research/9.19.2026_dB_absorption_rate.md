# Active Research Document: Topping Lite II → Edifier R1280DB Sound Level & Absorption Study

**Setup:**
- Source → USB → Topping Lite II DAC → RCA line-out → Edifier R1280DB (powered speakers, built-in amp)
- Goal: Understand how DAC output level (dB) affects perceived loudness, hearing exposure, and room/body sound absorption

---

## 1. Signal Chain Facts

- **Topping Lite II** uses a digital volume control on the DAC chip before the analog stage. Lowering its dB reduces the digital signal amplitude *before* it reaches the RCA outputs.
- **Edifier R1280DB** has its own analog volume knob on the amplifier, which is a *second, independent* gain stage.
- Because you have **two volume controls in series** (DAC digital volume + speaker amp analog volume), the same numeric dB reduction on the DAC does not correspond to a fixed SPL (sound pressure level) at your ears — it depends on where the Edifier knob is set too.

**Best practice for this chain (to preserve resolution/dynamic range):**
- Set the Topping Lite II near unity/0 dB (max digital volume) to avoid digital bit-depth loss.
- Do the *primary* volume control on the Edifier R1280DB analog knob instead.
- Only reduce DAC dB if you need to tame line-level signal to prevent clipping or distortion at the Edifier's input stage.

*(To confirm/measure: check Topping Lite II specs for line-out voltage — if it's higher than what the R1280DB's RCA input expects, you may need to lower a few dB to prevent input clipping. Note the manual's rated output voltage here once found.)*

---

## 2. What Lowering dB Actually Changes

| Lowered by | Effect |
|---|---|
| **DAC digital volume** | Reduces bits used to represent the signal digitally → theoretically reduces resolution/dynamic range at very low settings, but usually inaudible if reduction is small (a few dB) |
| **Speaker analog volume** | Reduces voltage sent to speaker drivers → reduces SPL output directly, no digital resolution loss |

**Key point:** Neither control changes the **absorption coefficient** of your body, furniture, or walls. Those are physical material properties (unitless coefficients from 0–1 per frequency) and stay constant regardless of DAC/amp settings. What changes is the **total acoustic energy** available to be absorbed, reflected, or transmitted.

---

## 3. Sound Pressure & Exposure Reference

- Every **-3 dB ≈ half the acoustic power** reaching your ears/room.
- Every **-10 dB ≈ one-tenth the acoustic power** (perceived as roughly "half as loud" to human hearing).
- NIOSH recommended exposure limit: **85 dBA average over 8 hrs**; time-halves every +3 dB above that. [(CDC/NIOSH)](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/noise/about/noise.html)
- Absorption formulas (Sabine equation) relate **room absorption (A, in sabins)** to reverberation time — but they don't care what your DAC dB is; they only care about actual SPL/energy present. [(Engineering Toolbox)](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/accoustic-sound-absorption-d_68.html)

## 5. Working Conclusions (update as you test)

1. Absorption rates of your body/room are **fixed physical properties** — lowering DAC dB only lowers the *energy* hitting them, not their *ability* to absorb.
2. For quality, keep the **DAC digital volume high (near 0 dB)** and use the **Edifier's analog knob** as primary volume control, unless you find the DAC is overdriving the amp's input.
3. Use an SPL meter for real-world dBA readings — a DAC's internal "dB" scale is *relative to its own max output*, not an absolute loudness measurement.
